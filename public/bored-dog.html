5 brain games that calm dogs down

A long walk tires your dog's legs. A few minutes of the right game tires its mind, and that's often what a restless dog is missing.

By Happy Dog Training TipsUpdated November 20266 min read

The short version

  • Many “bad” behaviors, like chewing, barking and pacing, come from a busy brain with nothing to do.
  • 5 to 10 minutes of a brain game can settle a dog better than an extra walk.
  • Start easy, reward often, and stop while your dog still wants more.

Why bored dogs act out

Dogs were bred to do jobs: herding, hunting, guarding, searching. Most of our dogs now spend the day waiting for us to come home. Their bodies may get a walk, but their brains get very little work.

When that mental energy has nowhere to go, it comes out as behavior we don't like. Chewing shoes, barking at every sound, following you from room to room and “zoomies” at night are often signs of a dog that needs something to think about.

The good news is that mental exercise is quick. Sniffing, searching and solving small puzzles use a lot of energy, so a short game can leave your dog relaxed and ready to nap.

The 5 games

You don't need special toys for any of these. Use small, soft treats or pieces of your dog's regular food.

  1. Find the treat

    Let your dog watch you put a treat under a cup or a folded towel. Say “find it!” and praise it when it gets the treat. Once that's easy, hide the treat while your dog waits in another room.

  2. The muffin tin puzzle

    Drop a treat into a few cups of a muffin tin and cover every cup with a tennis ball. Your dog has to move the balls to find the food. It's simple, cheap and surprisingly tiring.

  3. Which hand?

    Hold a treat in one closed hand and offer both fists. When your dog sniffs or touches the right hand, open it. If it paws or bites at the wrong one, calmly close both hands and try again.

  4. Name the toy

    Pick one toy and say its name every time you play with it, like “ball” or “bunny.” Ask your dog to “get ball” and reward it when it picks that toy. Some dogs learn the names of many toys this way.

  5. The sniff walk

    Once a day, slow your walk down and let your dog sniff as long as it likes. Sniffing is how dogs read the world, and a 15-minute sniff walk can be more tiring than a fast 30-minute one.

The golden ruleMake each game easy enough that your dog wins most of the time. A dog that feels successful wants to keep playing; a frustrated dog gives up.

A simple daily routine

You'll see the best results when brain games become a habit, not a one-time thing. Here's a routine most families can keep:

  • Morning: a 5-minute game of find the treat before you leave for the day.
  • After work: a sniff walk instead of a rushed walk.
  • Evening: the muffin tin puzzle or name the toy while dinner cooks.

Give it a week. Many owners notice a calmer dog in the evenings once its brain gets regular work.

Common questions

How long should a brain game last?
Start with 5 minutes. Some dogs can happily play for 10 to 15 minutes, but it's always better to stop while your dog is still excited than to push until it loses interest.
Can brain games replace walks?
No. Dogs need both. Walks give physical exercise and time outside; brain games add the mental work many dogs are missing. Together they make a calmer, happier dog.
My dog gets frustrated and gives up. What should I do?
Make the game easier. Use a smellier treat, let your dog watch you hide it, or use fewer cups. Then add difficulty slowly, only when your dog is succeeding every time.
